\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report documents the calibration of the course Vissim model of the signalised intersection
Kungsgatan and Slottsgatan in Norrk\"oping against mean speeds and accelerations measured on ten
weekday mornings at twelve locations, and the use of the calibrated model to evaluate three
alternative designs. Instead of the manual one-parameter-at-a-time procedure, the simulator was
driven automatically from Python through the Vissim COM interface. In total 1\,857 calibration runs
were performed in three phases. Each phase consisted of a space-filling Latin hypercube design, a
Gaussian-process (GP) surrogate for every measured quantity, a global sensitivity analysis based
on Sobol indices, a constrained optimisation on the surrogate, a local refinement design and an
independent validation with new random seeds.

Phase~1 calibrated on speeds only and reduced the speed RMSE from 4.53 to 1.34\kmh, but did so
through unrealistically gentle braking that destroyed the acceleration profile. Phase~2 calibrated
speeds and accelerations jointly with direction-specific desired speeds and is the recommended
model: the speed RMSE is halved to 2.01\kmh, the acceleration RMSE stays at the level of the
original model (0.13\mss), all four bicycle locations and the approaches L6 to L8 pass the course
$t$-test, the demand is fully served (maximum GEH 0.68) and the result holds separately on odd and
even field days. Phase~3 added further freedom and a test-oriented objective and confirmed that the
remaining discrepancies are structural rather than a matter of parameter values.

With 20 paired replications per scenario, an actuated signal controller reduces the average delay
by 26\,\% (21.3 to 15.7~s per vehicle) by moving green time from the lightly loaded side road to the
near-saturated main road. A 10-minute redistribution of demand caused by an incident creates
temporary queues on Slottsgatan that dissolve within the next interval. Raising the bicycle demand
to 800 per hour and direction reduces the car throughput on Kungsgatan by 7\,\% through the
conflict with right-turning vehicles and doubles the number of stops.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} microscopic traffic simulation, Vissim, calibration, surrogate model,
Gaussian process, Sobol sensitivity, actuated signal control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Background and motivation}

A microscopic traffic simulation model is only useful for decision making when it reproduces the
traffic behaviour that is observed at the site. Calibration adjusts the behavioural parameters of
the model until simulated and measured indicators agree within their natural variability;
validation then checks the calibrated model against data or random seeds that were not used for
the adjustment. Only after both steps can the model serve as a baseline against which alternative
designs are compared.

The Vissim lab of the course provides a model of the signalised intersection Kungsgatan and
Slottsgatan in Norrk\"oping together with mean speeds and accelerations measured on ten weekday
mornings. The lab instructions suggest calibrating by adjusting one parameter at a time, for
example the standstill distance, the additive and multiplicative safety distance, the desired speed
distributions and the desired acceleration and deceleration functions. With roughly fifteen
candidate parameters that interact non-linearly through queues, capacity and signal timing, such a
search covers only a tiny part of the parameter space and reveals nothing about interactions. This
work therefore treats the simulator as an expensive black-box function and replaces it by a
statistical surrogate model that can be analysed and optimised systematically.

\subsection{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Analyse the model in detail and identify every model element that can influence the
        measured speeds and accelerations.
  \item Automate the generation, simulation and evaluation of model variants.
  \item Run enough simulations of systematically varied parameter combinations to train a
        machine-learning surrogate of the simulator.
  \item Find optimal parameter values on the surrogate under explicit realism constraints.
  \item Validate the result with independent random seeds and the statistical tests of the course,
        and determine the required number of replications.
  \item Use the calibrated model to evaluate the three alternative designs of the lab: actuated
        signal control, an incident with time-varying demand and a strong increase of cycling.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Approach and contributions}

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a complete inventory of the behavioural elements of the network file, which revealed
        several mechanisms not named in the lab instructions (turning speeds, unused vehicle
        compositions, missing warm-up);
  \item a fully automated Python tool chain for variant generation, batch simulation through the
        Vissim COM interface and result extraction, which never modifies the original model;
  \item a surrogate-based calibration in three phases with GP models, Sobol sensitivity indices and
        constrained optimisation, using in total 1\,857 simulation runs;
  \item an explicit treatment of realism (served demand, GEH) and of statistical validity (warm-up,
        common random numbers, independent validation seeds, hold-out field days);
  \item a scenario analysis of the three alternative designs with 20 paired replications each.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Signal control and capacity}

The signal controller is a stage-based fixed-time controller (program~1, cycle time 90~s). Stage~1
serves Kungsgatan and its bicycles, stage~2 serves Slottsgatan. Decoding the controller data stored
in the network file gives an effective green time of about 48~s for Kungsgatan and 38~s for
Slottsgatan, with intergreen periods of 11~s and 14~s. With a saturation flow of about
1\,900~veh/h, the single northbound lane on Kungsgatan has a capacity of roughly
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{KN}} \approx 1\,900 \cdot \frac{48}{90} \approx 1\,010~\text{veh/h},
\end{equation}
while its straight and right-turning demand passing the stop line is 957~veh/h. This approach
therefore operates at a degree of saturation of about 0.95 and is very sensitive to every parameter
that changes the saturation flow. This observation shaped both the realism constraints of the
calibration (\cref{sec:constraints}) and the interpretation of the alternative designs.

\subsection{Field measurements}

The twelve measurement locations L1 to L12 correspond to the Vissim data collection measurements
1 to 12. Their exact positions were extracted from the network file (\cref{tab:locations}). The
position of a point relative to the stop line determines what it measures: exit points (L2, L3,
L5, L6) are dominated by acceleration towards the desired speed, approach points (L1, L4, L8) by
deceleration and queueing, and L7, which lies 2.1~m \emph{after} the southbound stop line,
measures the discharge of the queue. Left-turning vehicles leave the main lane into the turn
pocket upstream of L1, L4, L7 and L8, so at L1 and L8 the observed stream consists mostly of

\subsection{Overview of the calibration framework}

\Cref{fig:workflow} summarises the framework. A parameter vector is written into a stand-alone
network variant, simulated in batch through the COM interface, and evaluated against the field
data. The results of many variants train one GP surrogate per output. The surrogates are used for
sensitivity analysis and for a constrained optimisation, whose result is refined locally and
finally validated with independent random seeds. The validated model is then the baseline for the
alternative designs. The same loop was executed three times with different objectives and
parameterisations (\cref{sec:calibration}).

Two practical obstacles were solved during development. First, the original network belonged to a
Vissim scenario-management project. Such a file can only be loaded next to its project database
and cannot be simulated outside the ``Edit scenario'' mode, so every generated variant is written
as a stand-alone network (attribute \code{underScenMngm="false"}). Second, the result attributes
of the COM interface were verified with a smoke test before the large batches. The original
\code{Vissim\_lab.inpx} was never modified.

\subsection{Common simulation settings}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Fixed-time controller~1, program~1; demand and routes unchanged.
  \item Simulation period 2\,400~s: 600~s warm-up followed by 1\,800~s of data collection (one
        aggregation interval). The warm-up removes the bias of an initially empty network.
  \item Calibration runs: 3 replications per configuration with common random numbers (seed 42,
        increment 1), so that differences between configurations are not masked by random noise.
  \item Validation runs: 10 replications with independent seeds (seed 1001, increment 7).
  \item Simulation resolution 10 time steps per second (unchanged). One configuration with three
        replications took about 21~s in quick mode.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical comparison with the field data}

For every location the two-sample $t$-test of the course with pooled variance was applied,
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\bar x - \bar y}{\sqrt{s_p^2\left(\frac{1}{n_x}+\frac{1}{n_y}\right)}},\qquad
s_p^2 = \frac{(n_y-1)s_y^2 + (n_x-1)s_x^2}{n_x+n_y-2},
\label{eq:ttest}
\end{equation}
with $\bar y, s_y$ the field mean and standard deviation over $n_y = 10$ days and $\bar x, s_x$
the simulated mean and standard deviation over $n_x$ replications. The null hypothesis of equal
means is kept if $|T| < t_{0.975,\,n_x+n_y-2}$, which is 2.101 for the validation with $n_x = 10$.
The required number of replications follows the course formula
\begin{equation}
n = \left(\frac{t_{0.975,\,n_x-1}\, s_x}{\varepsilon\, \bar x}\right)^2, \qquad \varepsilon = 0.05 .
\label{eq:nrep}
\end{equation}
In addition, the root mean square error over locations,
\begin{equation}
\RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (\bar x_i-\bar y_i)^2},
\end{equation}
was computed separately for speeds ($m=12$) and accelerations ($m=8$).

\subsection{Realism constraints: the demand must be served}
\label{sec:constraints}

Larger safety distances lower the saturation flow. On the near-saturated northbound approach this
creates a queue that grows back to the network entry; vehicles can then no longer be generated and
the simulated flow at L4 falls below the demand. In such a model the approach speed at L4 drops,
which superficially improves the fit, but for the wrong reason. Two constraints were therefore
imposed on every candidate:
\begin{align}
\GEH_i &= \sqrt{\frac{2 (q_i^{\mathrm{sim}}-q_i^{\mathrm{exp}})^2}{q_i^{\mathrm{sim}}+q_i^{\mathrm{exp}}}} \le 5, \quad i = 1,\dots,8,\\
q_4^{\mathrm{sim}} &\ge 0.97\, q_4^{\mathrm{orig}},
\end{align}
where $q_i^{\mathrm{exp}}$ is the expected hourly flow of \cref{tab:locations} and
$q_4^{\mathrm{orig}}$ the flow at L4 in the original model. The second constraint is relative,
because even the uncongested original model counts slightly less than the theoretical demand at L4
(detector position, stochastic arrivals). On the surrogate a safety margin was used
($\GEH \le 4$ and a served flow one percentage point above the threshold).

\subsection{Experimental design}

Each phase started with a Latin hypercube sample (optimised for centred discrepancy) over the
parameter box, plus the original model and the parameterised baseline as references. After the
first surrogate optimisation, a refinement design of 40 points was drawn in a box of $\pm 25\,\%$
of the range around the surrogate optimum, together with the optimum itself, and the surrogate was
retrained on all data.

\subsection{Gaussian-process surrogate}

For each output (twelve speeds, eight flows and eight accelerations) an independent GP regression
model was fitted on the configuration means, with inputs scaled to $[0,1]^d$. The kernel is
\begin{equation}
k(\mathbf u,\mathbf u') = \sigma_f^2\, k_{\mathrm{Mat\acute ern},\,\nu=5/2}\!\left(\mathbf u,\mathbf u'; \boldsymbol\ell\right) + \sigma_n^2\,\delta(\mathbf u,\mathbf u'),
\end{equation}
with one length scale per parameter (automatic relevance determination), which also indicates how
strongly each output depends on each parameter. The white-noise term absorbs the simulation noise
of the three-replication means. The hyper-parameters were estimated by maximum marginal likelihood
(L-BFGS-B), and the predictive quality was assessed by five-fold cross-validation with the fitted
hyper-parameters.

\subsection{Global sensitivity analysis}

Sobol indices were estimated on the GP mean with the Saltelli sampling scheme and the Jansen
estimators ($N = 2\,048$ base samples). The total index $S_{T,j}$ is the share of the output
variance caused by parameter $j$, including all its interactions. Parameters with
$S_{T,j}\approx 0$ for all outputs can be fixed without loss.

\subsection{Optimisation on the surrogate}

The surrogate is cheap to evaluate, so a global search was used: $2^{15}$ Sobol points, followed by
five rounds of local search in which the 32 best points were perturbed with Gaussian noise of
decreasing width ($\sigma$ from 0.08 to 0.005 in scaled units, 8\,192 candidates per round).
Penalties enforce the constraints, and a very small term $0.05\,\overline{(\mathbf u-\mathbf u_0)^2}$
favours the solution closest to the original model when several are equally good. Three distinct
optima (differing by more than 5\,\% of the range in at least one parameter) were passed to the
validation, together with the best simulated configuration and the original model.

The original model, with the warm-up added, was simulated with the validation seeds. Its speeds at

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{L4 (+5.0\kmh) and L7 (+5.1\kmh):} approach speed and queue discharge are too fast.

The accelerations of the original model, on the other hand, are close to the field values (7 of 8

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Phase 1: calibration on speeds}
\label{sec:phase1}

\paragraph{Objective and design.} The objective was the speed RMSE over all twelve locations,
subject to the demand constraints. The screening design contained 160 Latin hypercube points plus
two references (486 runs), the refinement 41 points (123 runs) and the validation 5 configurations
with 10 replications (50 runs).

\paragraph{Surrogate and sensitivity.} The surrogate predicted all twelve speeds with a
cross-validated $R^2$ between 0.97 and 1.00 and the accelerations with $R^2$ between 0.87 and 0.99.
The sensitivity analysis gave a clear picture:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the right-turn speed dominates L4 ($S_T = 0.62$) and, together with the Slottsgatan desired
        speed, L8 and L1;
  \item the desired deceleration controls L1 ($S_T = 0.35$) and the accelerations at L1 and L8;
  \item the desired acceleration controls the queue discharge at L7 ($S_T = 0.64$) and the exits
        L2 and L5;
  \item the Kungsgatan desired speed controls the exits L3 and L6;
  \item the bicycle speeds depend only on the bicycle desired speed ($S_T \approx 0.97$); the
        e-bike offset is practically not identifiable, because only the flow-weighted mean desired
        speed matters;
  \item the conflict-area gaps and safety factors have no measurable influence on any output
        ($S_T < 0.01$) and were dropped from the later phases.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Result.} The validated phase-1 optimum reduced the speed RMSE from 4.53 to 1.34\kmh{}
and passed 6 of 12 speed tests. However, it achieved this with the desired deceleration and the
right-turn deceleration at their lower bounds (0.5 times the original and 0.5\mss) and an
acceleration scale of 1.18. Vehicles brake gently over more than 150~m and accelerate too hard, so
the acceleration profile is lost: the acceleration RMSE rose from 0.107 to 0.531\mss{} and only 1
of 8 acceleration tests was passed (for example $-0.82$ instead of $-1.79$\mss{} at L1). A model
that matches mean speeds through unrealistic dynamics is not a valid calibration.

\paragraph{Lesson.} The accelerations must be part of the calibration target.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Phase 2: joint calibration of speeds and accelerations}
\label{sec:phase2}

\paragraph{Changes compared with phase 1.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Objective.} Speed and acceleration errors were combined after normalisation with the
        median day-to-day standard deviation of the field data, $s_v = 0.560$\kmh{} and
        $s_a = 0.075$\mss:
        \begin{equation}
        J_2 = \frac{\RMSE_v}{s_v} + \frac{\RMSE_a}{s_a}.
        \end{equation}
        Both terms are thereby measured in the same unit, the typical day-to-day variation, so
        neither dominates.
  \item \textbf{Direction-specific desired speeds.} One Kungsgatan speed cannot match both L3
        (southbound exit, needs a higher speed) and L6 (northbound exit, needs a lower speed). Every
        vehicle input therefore received its own car and HGV composition and desired speed
        distribution: composition~1 for the northbound input, the existing composition~4 for the
        southbound input, the existing composition~5 for Slottsgatan east and a new composition~6
        for Slottsgatan west.
  \item \textbf{Ranges.} The ranges of parameters that had hit their bounds were widened, and the
        inactive conflict-area parameters and the unidentifiable e-bike offset were removed.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Result.} Screening (162 configurations), refinement (41) and validation (5
configurations, 10 runs) used 659 simulation runs. The validated optimum (\cref{tab:calibrated})
halves the speed RMSE of the original model (2.01\kmh) while keeping the acceleration RMSE at the
original level (0.131\mss). It passes 7 of 12 speed tests (L6, L7, L8 and all bicycle locations)
and 3 of 8 acceleration tests, i.e.\ 10 of 20 tests in total, the best of all validated models.

\paragraph{Trade-off between speeds and accelerations.} The phase-2 surrogate was optimised for
$\RMSE_v/s_v + w\,\RMSE_a/s_a$ with weights $w$ between 0 and 4 (\cref{fig:tradeoff}). The resulting
front shows a sharp conflict: below a speed RMSE of about 1.9\kmh{} the acceleration error rises
rapidly. The residuals along the front show that this conflict is caused almost entirely by L1.
Matching the field speed of 17.5\kmh{} at L1 requires gentle braking, while matching the field
deceleration of $-1.79$\mss{} requires firm braking, and the model cannot do both.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Phase 3: more freedom and a test-oriented objective}
\label{sec:phase3}

\paragraph{Changes compared with phase 2.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Objective in units of the $t$-test.} L1 has a field standard deviation of
        4.66\kmh, so a speed error of 3\kmh{} is statistically acceptable there, whereas at L3
        (0.34\kmh) an error of 0.7\kmh{} is not. Every error was therefore standardised with the
        field standard deviation of its own location, $z_i = (\bar x_i - \bar y_i)/s_{y,i}$, and
        penalised by a hinge-squared loss that is almost zero inside the acceptance band of the
        $t$-test and quadratic outside it:
        \begin{equation}
        J_3 = \frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{12} L(z_i^{v}) + \frac{1}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{8} L(z_i^{a}),\qquad
        L(z) = \max(0,|z|-0.75)^2 + 0.05\,z^2 .
        \end{equation}
        With $n_x = n_y = 10$ and similar simulated and field variances, the $t$-test passes
        approximately for $|z| < 0.9$, so $J_3$ directly rewards the number of passed tests.
  \item \textbf{Shape of the desired acceleration function.} In the field the vehicles leave the
        junction faster but accelerate less at the exit points than in the model, i.e.\ they
        accelerate harder at low speed and level off earlier. The acceleration function was
        therefore scaled with $\alpha_{\mathrm{lo}}$ up to 20\kmh{} and $\alpha_{\mathrm{hi}}$ from
        50\kmh, linearly interpolated in between.
  \item \textbf{Separate right-turn speeds} for vehicles from Kungsgatan (feeding exits L2 and L5)
        and from Slottsgatan (passing L1 and L8).
  \item \textbf{Reuse of all previous simulations.} The 404 configurations of phases 1 and 2 were
        mapped into the phase-3 parameter space (equal values in the new split parameters) and used
        as training data together with 163 new configurations, which reduced the new simulation
        effort to 539 runs.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Surrogate quality and sensitivity.} The surrogate trained on 567 configurations reached
cross-validated $R^2$ values between 0.976 and 1.00 for the speeds and between 0.91 and 0.99 for
the accelerations (\cref{tab:cv}). The sensitivity map (\cref{fig:sensitivity}) confirms that the
new parameters act where they were intended: $\alpha_{\mathrm{hi}}$ governs the exit acceleration at
L5 ($S_T = 0.70$), $\alpha_{\mathrm{lo}}$ the exit speeds at L5 and L7, the Kungsgatan right-turn
speed controls L4 ($S_T = 0.76$) and the served flow, and the Slottsgatan right-turn speed controls
L8.

\subsection{Experimental setup and traffic metrics}

Each scenario was simulated with 20 replications and the same random seeds as the baseline (seed
42, increment 1), 600~s warm-up and 1\,800~s of evaluation. With common random numbers, replication
$k$ of a scenario sees the same random arrivals as replication $k$ of the baseline, so differences
were tested with a paired $t$-test on the 20 replication-wise differences. The required number of
replications for the average delay (\cref{eq:nrep}, $\varepsilon = 5\,\%$) is 12 for the baseline,
16 for the actuated controller, 10 for the incident and 6 for the bicycle scenario, so 20
replications are sufficient.

The following metrics were chosen:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Average delay per vehicle} (vehicle network performance): the standard measure of
        the level of service of a signalised intersection and the quantity a signal control
        strategy aims to reduce.
  \item \textbf{Stops per vehicle} and \textbf{stopped delay}: indicators of driving comfort,
        emissions and the number of acceleration manoeuvres.
  \item \textbf{Mean and maximum queue length} at the ten queue counters (main lanes, left-turn
        pockets and both cycle tracks) in 10-minute intervals: queues show \emph{where} the delay
        occurs, whether the storage space is sufficient and how a temporary demand change builds up
        and dissolves.
  \item \textbf{Speeds and flows} at the twelve measurement locations: they connect the scenarios
        to the calibration and show the effect on the cycle tracks and on throughput.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Actuated signal control}

The actuated controller reduces the average delay of all vehicles by 5.6~s, from 21.3 to 15.7~s
per vehicle ($-26\,\%$, $p < 0.001$), the number of stops by 12\,\% and the stopped delay from 13.9
to 9.0~s. The improvement comes from Kungsgatan: the mean queue on the critical northbound lane
falls from 27.5 to 12.1~m and the southbound queue from 11.8 to 5.6~m, while the Slottsgatan queues
grow by 1 to 2~m. The fixed-time plan gives Slottsgatan 38~s of green although it carries only
about 20\,\% of the traffic; the actuated logic ends the Slottsgatan stage as soon as the gaps
exceed 3~s and gives the time to the heavily loaded main road.

The price is paid by the minor road: the approach speeds at L1 and L8 decrease (from 23.6 to 21.7
and from 19.2 to 16.8\kmh) because more Slottsgatan vehicles arrive on red. The cyclists on
Kungsgatan, who share stage~1, also benefit (bicycle queues halved). The total throughput is
unchanged.

\subsection{Incident with time-varying demand}

Over the whole 30 minutes the incident scenario does not differ from the baseline (delay $+0.1$~s,
$p = 0.67$), which is expected because the average demand is identical. The temporal analysis
(\cref{fig:altincident}) reveals the effect of the incident. In the second interval, when the
Slottsgatan demand increases to 370 and 380~veh/h, the left-turn queues on Slottsgatan double (east,
from 3.2 to 6.6~m) and almost triple (west, from 3.6 to 9.6~m). At the same time the reduced
Kungsgatan demand (920 instead of 1\,100~veh/h northbound) shortens the northbound queue slightly.
In the third interval all queues return to the baseline level.

The fixed-time plan therefore has enough reserve capacity on Slottsgatan to absorb the 10-minute
peak without residual queues. The critical approach remains Kungsgatan northbound, whose demand of
1\,100~veh/h in the first and last interval is close to its capacity in both scenarios.

\subsection{Increase of bicycle demand to 800 per direction}

The eightfold bicycle demand has two effects. For the cyclists themselves, the mean speed on the
cycle tracks decreases from 17.9 to 16.5\kmh{} (up to $-2.1$\kmh{} at the points before the
bicycle stop lines, L9 and L11), and the bicycle queues at the stop lines grow from about 1 to
10~m (maximum 57~m).

For motorised traffic, the right-turning vehicles from Kungsgatan must yield to a nearly continuous
stream of cyclists. The flow that passes L4 and L6 drops from 943 to 878~veh/h and from 999 to
945~veh/h ($-7\,\%$); the northbound queue grows from 27.5 to 35.3~m and the southbound left-turn
queue from 8.5 to 12.6~m. The number of stops per vehicle doubles (0.74 to 1.51, mainly bicycle
stops at red), and the average delay rises by 1.2~s per vehicle. This average underestimates the
effect on cars, because the many bicycles with moderate delay enter the average and because the
vehicles that can no longer enter the network on Kungsgatan are not counted. The scenario thus
shows that the conflict between right-turning cars and cyclists, not the bicycle signal itself, is
the capacity bottleneck at higher bicycle volumes.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================

\paragraph{Value of the surrogate approach.} The 1\,857 calibration runs were sufficient to map a 14- to
16-dimensional parameter space with a predictive accuracy of $R^2 \ge 0.97$ for all speeds. The
surrogate made it possible to compute global sensitivity indices, to optimise under constraints in
seconds, to explore the trade-off between conflicting targets and to reuse all earlier runs when
the parameterisation was extended. A one-at-a-time search would not have revealed the interaction
between car following, right-turn speed and capacity at L4, nor the fact that the speed-only
optimum relies on unrealistic deceleration.

\paragraph{Choice of the calibration target.} The three phases show that the result depends at least
as much on the objective as on the parameters. Fitting speeds alone gives the lowest speed RMSE but
an invalid model. Adding accelerations restores the dynamics. Weighting each location by its own
field variability corresponds to the statistical test used for acceptance but cannot overcome
structural conflicts. A calibration report should therefore always state the objective and show
more than one performance measure.

\paragraph{Structural limitations.} Two conflicts remain in every phase: the approach at L1 (low
speed together with strong deceleration) and the exits, which are slightly too slow while the model
already accelerates harder than observed. Both point to the representation of turning movements
(reduced-speed areas only on the connectors, after the stop line) and to the use of one desired
acceleration function for all situations. Possible improvements outside the scope of parameter
calibration are reduced-speed areas or desired speed decisions upstream of the stop lines, separate
vehicle types or driving behaviours for turning traffic, and a check of the detector positions in
the network against the field measurement positions.

\paragraph{Demand constraint.} Without the served-demand constraint the optimiser preferred
configurations in which up to 30\,\% of the northbound demand could not enter the network, because
the resulting congestion lowers the speed at L4. Checking GEH and unfinished vehicle inputs is
therefore an essential part of the calibration and not only of the validation.

\paragraph{Reliability of the scenario results.} The scenario results are only as reliable as the
calibrated model in the situations it was calibrated for. The actuated controller and the incident
change the timing and the demand, which the calibration covers well. The bicycle scenario relies on
the interaction between cyclists and right-turning cars at the conflict areas, whose parameters
could not be calibrated because they had no influence on the measured speeds at 100 bicycles per
hour; this result should therefore be regarded as more uncertain.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The field data provide no flow counts, so the demand of the model was taken
as correct. The surrogate optimum is only as good as the surrogate; this was controlled by
cross-validation and by validating several distinct optima with independent seeds. The bicycle
speeds identify only the flow-weighted mean desired speed of bicycles and e-bikes, so the split
between the two types was fixed. Per-class network results (cars, HGV, bicycles) could not be
extracted through the COM interface with the available licence, so the scenario comparison uses
all-vehicle metrics together with bicycle speeds and queues.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
%=====================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A fully automated calibration chain (model variant generation, Vissim COM control, result
        extraction, GP surrogates, sensitivity analysis, constrained optimisation and validation)
        was developed and applied in three phases with 1\,857 simulation runs.
  \item The original model deviates mainly at the approaches (L1, L4, L7, L8) and on the cycle
        tracks; with warm-up it passes 2 of 12 speed tests and has a speed RMSE of 4.53\kmh.
  \item The most influential parameters are the right-turn speed, the Slottsgatan and Kungsgatan
        desired speeds, the desired acceleration and deceleration functions and the bicycle desired
        speed. The conflict-area gap parameters have no influence.
  \item Calibrating on speeds only (phase~1) produces an invalid model with unrealistic dynamics.
  \item The recommended model (phase~2) halves the speed RMSE to 2.01\kmh, keeps the acceleration
        RMSE at 0.13\mss, passes 10 of 20 tests including all bicycle locations, serves the demand
        (GEH $\le 0.68$) and is stable on independent seeds and on odd and even field days.
  \item The remaining discrepancies are structural; additional parameter freedom (phase~3) does not
        improve the test results further.
  \item The approach points require 10 to 14 replications for a relative error of 5\,\%; at least
        15 replications are recommended for the analysis of alternative designs.
  \item Alternative designs (20 paired replications): the actuated controller reduces the average
        delay by 5.6~s per vehicle ($-26\,\%$) and the main-road queues by more than half; the
        incident demand causes only temporary side-road queues; 800 bicycles per direction reduce
        the car throughput on Kungsgatan by 7\,\% and double the stops.
\end{enumerate}

%=====================================================================
\section{Reflections}
\label{sec:reflections}
%=====================================================================

The exercise illustrates several general points about microscopic traffic simulation.

\paragraph{A model is a hypothesis.} Calibration tests a hypothesis about behaviour against data.
The phases above show that a good fit of one indicator (speeds) can hide a wrong mechanism (braking
behaviour), so several independent indicators are needed.

\paragraph{Stochasticity must be handled explicitly.} Warm-up, the number of replications, common
random numbers for comparisons and independent seeds for validation all changed the conclusions
noticeably.

\paragraph{Simulation as a black-box function.} Replacing the simulator by a statistical surrogate
turns calibration into a well-defined optimisation and sensitivity problem, which makes the process
transparent and reproducible. The choice of the objective, however, remains an engineering decision
that has to be justified.

\paragraph{The value of a calibrated model lies in comparison.} The absolute numbers of every
scenario carry the remaining calibration error, but the paired differences between scenarios,
obtained under identical random conditions, are precise and robust.

\subsection{Controlling Vissim from Python}

\Cref{lst:com} shows the essential COM calls used by \code{run\_vissim.py} and \code{run\_alt.py}.
Vissim is started once per batch and every variant is loaded into the same instance.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Essential Vissim COM calls (simplified).},label={lst:com}]
import win32com.client as com

vissim = com.Dispatch("Vissim.Vissim")          # start or attach to Vissim
vissim.LoadNet(r"C:\Temp\run\variant.inpx", False)

sim = vissim.Simulation
sim.SetAttValue("NumRuns", 10)                  # replications
sim.SetAttValue("RandSeed", 1001)               # first seed
sim.SetAttValue("RandSeedIncr", 7)              # seed increment
sim.SetAttValue("UseMaxSimSpeed", True)
vissim.Graphics.CurrentNetworkWindow.SetAttValue("QuickMode", 1)

vissim.SuspendUpdateGUI()
try:
    sim.RunContinuous()                         # all replications
finally:
    vissim.ResumeUpdateGUI()

for dcm in vissim.Net.DataCollectionMeasurements.GetAll():
    loc = dcm.AttValue("No")
    for run in range(1, 11):
        v = dcm.AttValue(f"SpeedAvgArith({run},1,All)")   # km/h
        a = dcm.AttValue(f"Acceleration({run},1,All)")    # m/s^2
        n = dcm.AttValue(f"Vehs({run},1,All)")            # vehicles
\end{lstlisting}
